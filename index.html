This fall

The college audition season is already open, and the fall dates are the tight ones. Muhlenberg’s only on‑campus theatre and dance audition day this cycle is Saturday, October 24, 2026; their music audition day is not until February 21. That gap matters, because Muhlenberg asks you to audition no later than your own application deadline so the scholarship can be reflected in your first financial‑aid offer — and their Early Decision I deadline is November 1, Early Action December 1. If you are applying early, the audition happens in October or it happens too late to count.

Moravian publishes no audition dates at all — you schedule one by calling the music department — and the paperwork closes before the audition does: their information form is due two weeks ahead, and if you want the accompanist they provide, your two selections have to be in their hands two weeks ahead as well. Locally — Moravian, Muhlenberg, West Chester — you audition in person, usually two contrasting pieces, often with a foreign‑language selection expected, and none of the three asks for a prescreen video.

A recorded prescreen is a top‑conservatory and musical‑theatre requirement rather than a general one — the two I can currently source are Michigan on December 1 and Temple Musical Theatre on December 15. If your list includes schools like those, the recording gets made in October.

Either way the repertoire has to be chosen and memorised now, not in January.

What I do for a senior: choose repertoire against what that school actually asks for — the requirements genuinely differ, and this is the step families get wrong — get it into your voice, get legible sheet music in your key to their accompanist by their deadline, and record the video to spec for the schools that want one. No extra charge for the recording session if you study here.

Who I am

I am a classically trained dramatic tenor. I studied voice at Carnegie Mellon, where I took a minor in vocal performance and was cast as the lead in the voice department’s opera — as a non‑major, in a department full of people whose entire degree was singing.

Before that I won multiple NATS competitions between the ages of sixteen and eighteen, and sang twice at Carnegie Hall, in choirs, before I was twenty. Since then I have spent roughly twelve years being paid to sing — in a church choir, and at weddings and funerals, which is where you learn to walk in cold and be correct on the first take.

I still study. My teacher of the last decade is Harold Evans, who has conducted Pavarotti and coached Domingo, and whose students include performers who went on to lead roles on Broadway. What I teach is a real lineage with names you can look up — not a method I invented.

What a lesson is

Forty‑five minutes, weekly. We warm up, we work technique, and we work repertoire — usually in that order, and usually with more time on technique than you expect in the first two months.

I play your exercises live rather than running a track, so the warm‑up can move where your voice actually is that day instead of where it was last Tuesday. You leave with a recording of the exercises we did, so the week’s practice sounds like the lesson.

What I am listening for, in order: whether the breath is doing the work, whether the tone is free, whether the vowel is honest, and only then whether the notes are right. Most problems that present as range problems are not range problems.

If you are here because somebody else wants you to be, we will both be able to tell by the third lesson, and I’ll say so.

Online lessons

I teach online and I will tell you honestly how it works, because most people selling it will not.

You cannot sing together over the internet. There is around a second of delay in each direction and no amount of good equipment removes it — it is the speed of light and the routers in between. Any teacher who plays live piano while you sing over video is giving you a lesson where one of you is wrong about where the beat is.

So I do not do that. I record your exercises and your accompaniment in advance and send them to you. You play them on your end, at your end’s tempo, and sing with them. I hear your voice, clean, with nothing lagging behind it. Everything else — listening, correcting, demonstrating, talking — is turn‑taking anyway, and turn‑taking is fine over a second of delay.

I spent thirty‑one years in information technology before I did this full‑time. The delay is not a mystery to me and I am not going to pretend it isn’t there.
